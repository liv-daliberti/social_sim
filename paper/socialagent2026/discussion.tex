\section{Discussion: why inductive reasoning matters}
\label{sec:discussion}

Forecasting is our testbed for inductive reasoning, not the boundary of the
claim. It makes otherwise hidden choices observable: which evidence matters,
which population offers the relevant comparison, when local behavior should
override context, and whether a relationship survives a new world. Each choice
implies a different numerical forecast. The same ability matters whenever a
social agent must generalize from sparse evidence rather than repeat a familiar
pattern.

In practice, a forecast is rarely the last question. Users need to know which
evidence should change a decision, whether the conclusion survives in a different
population, and what observation should trigger revision. A model that reaches
the right number through a topical cue or a familiar base rate may still be
unable to support those judgments. Average
accuracy can hide that weakness precisely because most evaluation cases resemble
the past. It becomes costly when a decision concerns a new group, an unusual
institution, or a policy that changes the usual pathway.

We designed the experiments around alternatives left open by the preceding
study. The market study tests whether forecasts ignore topical chatter and follow
evidence up or down, but leaves open a verbal shortcut. Coin City makes the hidden
comparison visible; conflicting local cases test revision, arbitrary labels
remove familiar semantics, and an intervention connects the induced
representation to behavior. Transfer to a new domain and response pathway then
tests whether the result is specific to one prompt, while historical markets test
accuracy outside training. No single step is sufficient; together, these
successively stronger contrasts narrow the explanations for success.

These distinctions extend beyond forecasting. A persuasion model should respond to
both a message and who delivers it. A coordination model should distinguish
shared payoffs from what participants know about one another. A policy model
should distinguish similar language from the institutional pathway through which
an effect occurs. In synthetic populations, these paired changes can reveal
whether conditional relationships survive even when aggregate averages look
human-like
\citep{park2023generative,aher2023simulate,argyle2023out,bisbee2024synthetic}.
For social agents in particular, the relationship is often the object of
interest: who knows what, which group treats a message as credible, or which
institution transmits an action into an outcome. Matching a population average
while missing those dependencies can produce a convincing static simulation and
a misleading response to intervention.

For evaluation, this implies measuring both outcomes and reasons for change. Hold
surface content fixed while changing the relevant analogue;
provide local evidence that contradicts context; replace meaningful cues with a
new convention; and test the relationship again after the setting changes.
Headline accuracy and parameter count cannot substitute for these checks because
the relevant capability depends on the population, pathway, and decision at hand.

\section{Limitations: what these studies do and do not establish}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evidence supports a specific claim: models use context-dependent predictive
relationships in these tasks. It does not establish human-like understanding or
a general internal world model. The market packets are controlled pieces of
text, Coin City offers two simple response patterns, and the transfer study spans
two constructed worlds. Real settings contain strategic interaction,
unreliable cues, changing variables, and feedback over longer horizons.

The internal result is narrower still. Decoding identifies a representation
associated with the selected convention, and one intervention shows that this
representation affects one checkpoint's forecast. That is stronger than a
correlation alone, but not a claim about every model or every form of reasoning.
Likewise, the historical study covers one market cohort and training recipe.
Additional architectures, later cohorts, interactive settings, and deliberately
misleading evidence would test how far the pattern extends.

Performance is heterogeneous. Smaller checkpoints often struggle when the
KIV/ZOR meaning must be induced anew, and historical-market gains narrow at the
strongest Qwen base. Because the scale comparison uses one training recipe across
model sizes, its nonmonotonic shape should not be read as a law of capacity:
larger models may need different learning rates, schedules, or amounts of data.
The sequence is useful partly because it shows where performance changes rather
than collapsing inductive reasoning into one yes-or-no score.

\section{Ethics, artifacts, and conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The studies use public market data, model outputs, synthetic worlds, and an unpaid
adult review of synthetic materials. No identifying or sensitive information was
collected. Counterfactual packets were evaluated offline, never posted to markets,
and never used for trading. We report aggregate behavior rather than recommend a
deployment or trading strategy.

An anonymized artifact contains prompts, fixed datasets, saved model responses,
analysis code, and figure builders.
The appendix records the implementation and statistical details omitted from the
main narrative.

Forecast accuracy is a starting point, not a sufficient test of inductive
reasoning. Stronger tests ask whether a model notices relevant evidence, moves in
the implied direction, chooses and revises comparisons, infers new conventions,
and preserves relationships under change. The larger tested models pass these
contrasts, subject to the limits above. For social agents, these experiments test
both real-world accuracy and relationship-sensitive updating without presuming a
general theory of the world.
