\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A forecast of a social event is a compact theory of how a world works. News of a
policy proposal, an endorsement, or an economic shock matters only through a
relationship: which actors can respond, which institutions transmit the effect,
and whether the local setting amplifies or dampens it. The same observation can
therefore support different predictions in different contexts. A national story
may move opinion in a media environment organized around national politics and
barely register where local issues dominate. Forecasting requires selecting the
relationship that applies before extrapolating from the evidence.

This requirement matters as language models (LMs) become components of social
agents. LMs already populate interactive worlds, emulate experimental
participants, and generate synthetic public opinion
\citep{park2023generative,aher2023simulate,argyle2023out}. Their outputs can look
plausible in aggregate while missing the conditional relationships that a social
scientist or policy designer actually wishes to study
\citep{bisbee2024synthetic}. A system that reproduces an average response but
reacts to the wrong cue, or carries a relationship into a setting where it should
change, is a fragile social model.

Forecasting offers a demanding test of this problem. Competitive accuracy alone
does not reveal the process behind a probability. The same number can be produced
by a base rate, a retrieved precedent, a market price, a lexical shortcut, or a
relationship inferred from the current context. Generated rationales do not
resolve the ambiguity because explanations need not track model behavior
\citep{jacovi2020towards,turpin2023language}. We therefore ask a process question:
\emph{when an LM forecasts a social outcome, does it behave as if it selects,
revises, and transfers predictive structure?}

We use \emph{predictive structure} for relationships among evidence, context, and
outcomes, and \emph{contextual structural induction} for three behavioral
operations: selecting a relationship from context and sparse examples, revising
that selection as local evidence accumulates, and applying it to a new case
\citep{tenenbaum2006theory,griffiths2010probabilistic,lake2017building}.
The definition is deliberately behavioral. It presumes neither an explicit
causal graph nor a general internal world model.

The experiments follow this ladder (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-ladder}). On
unresolved real markets, we first ask whether models separate outcome-bearing
evidence from topical chatter and update in the implied direction. We then build
Coin City, a controlled public-opinion world in which media context selects one
of two displayed response relationships. Reversing that context should reverse
the forecast; adding target-city observations should weaken it. Arbitrary labels
whose meaning changes every episode test whether models infer a local convention
rather than rely on familiar words. Representation analysis and activation
patching ask whether the same distinction is present inside open checkpoints.
Finally, two training studies ask whether relationship use transfers across
surface and mechanism shifts and whether proper-score training improves forecasts
on sealed historical events.

The resulting claim is narrower than the claim that LMs have world models and
more useful for evaluating social agents. Across these settings, forecasts behave
as if they use context-dependent relationships, but the behavior is not universal
across families or scales. The contribution is therefore both empirical and
methodological: aggregate realism should be supplemented with interventions that
change the relationship while holding easier cues fixed.
