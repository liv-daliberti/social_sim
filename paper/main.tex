\begin{abstract}

Judgmental forecasting involves predicting uncertain events like elections or geopolitical conflicts where
abundant historical data is lacking.
A key challenge is \textit{induction}: forecasters must generalize from sparse evidence and contextual knowledge to infer how available evidence predicts future outcomes.
Here, we investigate whether language models (LMs) recover and use such predictive relationships when forecasting, rather than relying on base rates or surface cues.
Across a battery of experiments spanning real-world prediction markets and controlled synthetic environments, we test whether models: 1) update their beliefs coherently; 2) correctly recover predictive relationships, and 3) transfer what they learn to new settings.
Our results provide evidence for each capability.
Models selectively update their forecasts in response to causally relevant evidence, use contextual information to infer predictive relationships when observations are sparse, and revise these inferences as direct evidence accumulates.
Training on families of predictive relationships improves forecasting on unseen structures, although the gains diminish as the held-out structures depart further from those seen in training.
Together, our experiments provide behavioral evidence that LMs can forecast inductively.
\footnotemark
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Judgmental forecasting is a demanding form of reasoning central to decisions about political, social, and economic futures.
Unlike settings with abundant historical data for statistical extrapolation, judgmental forecasting requires integrating sparse, noisy evidence with contextual knowledge to form calibrated beliefs about future outcomes \citep{tetlock2016superforecasting,mellers2014psychological,lawrence2006judgmental}.
This creates an inductive challenge: forecasters must infer from limited observations the relationships that govern how evidence impacts outcomes, and use those relationships to anticipate how events might unfold \citep{tenenbaum2006theory,griffiths2010probabilistic,tenenbaum2011mind,lake2017building}.
Figure~\ref{fig:world-knowledge-evidence} illustrates this identification problem in a toy setting.
A researcher observes a carnival coin-flipping machine and, after seeing two heads, must estimate the probability that the next flip will be heads.
A judgmental forecast may incorporate contextual knowledge (e.g., that such games are often rigged) to infer the data-generating process.

Language models (LMs) now approach crowd accuracy in judgmental forecasting \citep{halawi2024approaching,schoenegger2024wisdom}, but accurate predictions need not imply recovery of the underlying task structure.
Models recover latent structure in some settings \citep{li2023emergent}, yet can predict accurately using relationships that fail to generalize in others \citep{vafa2025foundation}.
This distinction matters when decision-makers ask how and whether a forecast should change under alternative evidence or events.
Answering such questions requires predictive relationships that generalize across changes in context.

\noindent
\textbf{Present work.}
We study whether LMs exhibit inductive reasoning in judgemental forecasting tasks through a series of progressively more stringent behavioral tests spanning both real-world prediction markets and controlled synthetic environments.
First, we evaluate whether LMs respond selectively to new evidence, distinguishing outcome-relevant information from topically related but orthogonal information (\hyperref[sec:exp1]{Exp.~1}).
Second, we test whether LMs infer predictive relationships from described context before statistical evidence accumulates.
We construct synthetic forecasting problems in which the numerical observations are identical across conditions and only the described setting varies, isolating the contribution of background knowledge to the LM's forecast (\hyperref[sec:exp2]{Exp.~2}).
Finally, we test whether relationships learned during fine-tuning transfer to new settings.
We fine-tune LMs with reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) on both synthetic tasks and historical prediction market data, diagnose component failures with a supervised pilot, and evaluate on held-out structures and domains (\hyperref[sec:exp3]{Exp.~3}, \hyperref[sec:exp4]{Exp.~4}).

\noindent\textbf{Results.}
Across experiments, we find behavioral evidence that models forecast inductively.
\vspace{-1.5mm}
\begin{itemize}[
    leftmargin=3.5mm,
    itemsep=2pt,
    parsep=0pt,
    topsep=3pt
]
\item When forecasting unresolved prediction-market questions, all nine tested models selectively incorporate new evidence, revising their forecast probabilities $1.9$–$7.5\times$ more for outcome-relevant than for merely topically related information (\hyperref[sec:exp1]{Exp.~1}).
\item In controlled synthetic worlds, we find that models use context to select between predictive relationships even when the numerical evidence is held constant, and increasingly rely on direct observations as they accumulate, as a forecaster capable of inductive reasoning should (\hyperref[sec:exp2]{Exp.~2}).
We further find that larger models can learn a new relationship from examples within the prompt. For example, when told that a city resembles either KIV or ZOR (random strings), 12 of 15 tested models can infer from examples how each city type responds to events in the simulated environment.
For Qwen3-14B, manipulating the internal representation of this inferred relationship (resemblance to KIV or ZOR) changes the model's forecasts, providing causal evidence that the inferred relationship guides its prediction.
\item Finally, we find that post-training can teach models predictive relationships that generalize across domains, although evidence for more demanding forms of transfer is substantially weaker.
On synthetic data, post-training transfers robustly across a change in domain, but evidence for transfer when both the domain and the relationship between evidence and outcomes change is weaker: for Qwen3-8B, the episode-matched advantage is $0.126$ (95\% CI $[-0.002, 0.265]$) across eight seeds (\hyperref[sec:exp3]{Exp.~3}).
On 318 held-out real-world prediction markets, training on other event families reduces mean forecasting error at every tested model size, with several trained checkpoints reaching contemporaneous crowd performance (\hyperref[sec:exp4]{Exp.~4}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Do forecasts change for the right reason?}
\label{sec:evidence}

Appropriately revising beliefs as new evidence arrives is a hallmark of strong judgmental forecasters \citep{tetlock2005expert}.
We argue that effective updating requires a forecaster
to determine:
(1)~which evidence is relevant in predicting an outcome and (2)~the
extent to which the new evidence should affect the forecast.
Figure~\ref{fig:exp1-pipeline} illustrates these challenges considering the question of whether the Trump administration will order a federal
review of new AI models. For (1), new evidence that corroborates plans for such a review should change the forecast, whereas other related AI news should not do so (unless there is a plausible causal pathway connecting it to the outcome).
For (2), the forecaster must determine the extent to which the new evidence should affect the outcome probability. Both challenges require inductive reasoning, as they require the forecaster to infer the predictive structure connecting observed evidence to future outcomes.

We assess these capacities in two experiments.
First, we pair real-world prediction-market questions with controlled synthetic evidence to test whether models update selectively and in the direction the evidence warrants.
Second, we use a controlled synthetic environment where the same evidence has, by construction,  different predictive effects across contexts. This let us test whether models can infer the relationships determining how strongly the evidence should affect forecasts.

\subsection{Exp.~1: Can models update in the right direction?}
\label{sec:exp1}

\noindent\textbf{Design.}
We evaluate the forecasting capabilities of nine models on 100 unresolved binary Polymarket questions
with web search. Then, we ask the models to revise their forecast after a fictional news update with
search disabled (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1-pipeline}). Unbeknownst to the models, we construct these updates to support the outcome, oppose it, or cover the same topic without directly influencing the outcome.
Because models could potentially distinguish directional from orthogonal
evidence using superficial textual cues,  we additionally evaluate a word-only, bag-of-words classifier that receives only the text of the news update.
We also construct matched forecasting scenarios in which the same evidence should produce opposed outcomes; for example, intense rainfall could increase the probability of a good harvest in a dry region but decrease it in a region prone to flooding.
(See  App.~\ref{app:exp1} for details.)

\noindent\textbf{Finding: models selectively update in the direction implied by the evidence.}
Across the nine models considered, and 30,094 valid forecast revisions, we find that pro-outcome evidence increases forecasts, anti-outcome evidence decreases them, and topically related but orthogonal evidence produces little change (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1-updating}a).
Conditional on changing their forecast, we find that models move their probability $1.9$--$7.5\times$ farther for updates intended to influence the prediction (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1-updating}b).
Uncertainty estimates and robustness analyses are reported in App.~\ref{app:exp1-threshold-robustness}; independent human review covers the original evidence packets.

\noindent\textbf{Finding: lexical cues alone do not explain model performance.}
We find that bag-of-words classifiers can almost perfectly distinguish directional from orthogonal evidence ($.989$ accuracy), confirming that superficial cues could explain models' selectivity.
Yet, the same classifiers are much less successful at distinguishing whether directional evidence supports or opposes the outcome ($.688$ accuracy).
Models are considerably more directionally consistent ($.887$--$.990$), suggesting lexical cues alone cannot explain their performance.
More directly, we hold the evidence, the prior, and the named actor fixed and vary only the stated relationship to the outcome. Because the three correct answers within such a triple are distinct, any predictor that ignores the relationship is bounded at one third of items and no complete triple; four such predictors, including one permitted to choose the best constant revision knowing every correct answer, score exactly $320/960$ items and $0/320$ triples. GPT-5.6 reverses $48/48$ triples and matches the exact posterior on $144/144$ items (App.~\ref{app:exp1-fresh-factorial}).

\noindent\textbf{Finding: selective updating depends on inference-time reasoning.}
Having ruled out simple lexical shortcuts, we next ask what makes this capability possible. In particular, does the model simply recognize a familiar pattern, or does it need inference-time reasoning to combine the stated relationship with the evidence? We isolate this by holding the model weights, prompts, and test items fixed and changing only whether reasoning is enabled. We find that Qwen3-32B's success on fixed evidence rises from 9.1\% to 92.8\%, while exact-posterior accuracy rises from 0.1\% to 84.5\%.
Since the two conditions see identical items, this gap isolates the reasoning setting rather than item difficulty or information available in the prompt (App.~\ref{app:exp1-fresh-factorial}).

\noindent\textbf{Take-away.}
We find that models distinguish evidence that is predictive of an outcome from information that is only topically related, and generally update in the direction implied by that evidence. Models' performance is not explained by surface-level lexical cues alone. This ability largely disappears when inference-time reasoning is disabled, suggesting that models must reason about how evidence bears on the outcome rather than just recognizing familiar cues.

\subsection{Exp.~2: can models infer how strongly evidence should matter?}
\label{sec:exp2}

\noindent\textbf{Design.}
We study whether models can determine \textit{how much} new evidence should update a forecast in a synthetic scenario we dub \emph{Coin City}.
We construct two reference cities (Cities A and B) where news coverage affects election polling differently: strongly in one city and weakly in the other.
Specifically, an election poll in a city changes based on whether the news coverage favored one of the candidates ($x_{ij} \in [-10,-4] \cup [4,10]$) and the city's sensitivity to news, drawing $g_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0.90,0.03^2)$ for the strong-response city and $g_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0.25,0.03^2)$ for the weak-response city:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta p_{ij}=g_j x_{ij}+\epsilon_{ij},
  \qquad \epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,5^2).
  \label{eq:coin-city-dgp}
\end{equation}

We then ask the model to forecast the effect of campaign news on polling in a third city, City C, which we construct to have either the strong or weak response. Across conditions, we incrementally provide the model with additional information, asking it each time to forecast how a new piece of campaign news will affect election polling in City C. Specifically, we provide

\vspace{-1mm}
\begin{itemize}[
    leftmargin=5.5mm,
    labelsep=1.5mm,
    itemsep=2pt,
    parsep=0pt,
    topsep=1pt,
    partopsep=0pt
]
\item[\textcolor{cityCblue}{\textbf{(a)}}]
Up to four previous observations from City C, showing how polling numbers
changed in response to news.

\item[\textcolor{refgreen}{\textbf{(b)}}]
Additionally, four observations from each of Cities A and B, showing how
their polling numbers changed in response to news.

\item[\textcolor{cuepurple}{\textbf{(c)}}]
Additionally, a contextual cue: strong-response cities are described as
primarily receiving national news coverage and weak-response, local news coverage.

\item[\textcolor{cuepurple}{\textbf{(d)}}]
Building on (b), we provide an \textit{incorrect} contextual cue, linking
City C to the wrong type of city.

\item[\textcolor{cuepurple}{\textbf{(e)}}]
Finally, we replace the semantic cues with \textit{arbitrary} labels
(KIV and ZOR), requiring the model to infer their meanings.
\end{itemize}

We illustrate our incremental experimental setup in Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-prompt}.

This design allows us to test whether models use context to infer how strongly new evidence should affect their forecasts, and whether they increasingly rely on direct evidence from City C as it becomes available---all with ground truth on how forecasts should update.
We evaluate six frontier systems: Claude Opus~4.8 \citep{anthropic2026claude48}, Opus~5 \citep{anthropic2026claude5}, GPT-5.6 \citep{openai2026gpt56},
DeepSeek~V4-Pro \citep{deepseek2026v4}, Kimi~K3 \citep{moonshot2026kimik3}, and Gemini~3.6~Flash \citep{google2026gemini36}.
As baselines, we also fit OLS regressions to the observations available in each
condition to estimate the relationship between campaign news and polling changes.


\noindent\textbf{Finding: models use context to make better forecasts.}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-results}, we show that when there are few observed City C cases, strong models use the reference cities together with the contextual cue to infer which response pattern applies.
For stronger models, this ``hint'' substantially improves forecasting over relying on City C alone or pooling the reference cities without knowing which one is relevant; e.g., in Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-results}c, GPT-5.6 Sol substantially outperforms the baselines when $k=0$ or $k=1$.
This holds true even when the cues given are arbitrary KIV/ZOR labels whose meaning must be inferred (Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-results}e).
Finally, giving the model the \textit{wrong} contextual cue  sharply worsens its forecast (Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-results}d).

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{exp2_causal_patch_swap.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Changing the inferred KIV/ZOR relationship changes Qwen3-14B's forecast.}
    With no direct City C evidence, replacing the label-token hidden states with
    those produced by the opposite-label prompt moves the forecast toward the
    opposite response regime. Patching three mid-network layers moves it as far
    as overwriting the label token itself.}
    \label{fig:coin-city-causal-patch}
\end{wrapfigure}

\noindent\textbf{Finding: models revise their initial contextual inference as direct evidence accumulates.}
We find that context matters most when direct evidence from City C is sparse.
As observations from City C accumulate, forecasts increasingly approach the OLS baselines. This suggests the models shift from contextual information toward direct evidence as its evidential value grows, as a forecaster should.
This pattern holds across conditions in Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-results}, but is most striking in Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-results}d: even when initially misled by an incorrect contextual cue, models recover as direct evidence accumulates.

\noindent\textbf{Mechanistic analysis.}
The above behavioral results show that models can infer which predictive relationship applies, \textit{but they do not tell us whether the model represents that relationship internally or uses it to produce the forecast.}
We thus further investigate how the predictive relationship inferred from the arbitrary KIV/ZOR labels is represented internally and whether that representation actually influences the forecast.
We fit linear probes to hidden states from Qwen3-14B~\citep{qwen2025qwen3} and Llama-3.1-70B~\citep{grattafiori2024llama} to decode whether the model associates City C with the strong- or weak-response regime.
We then test whether this representation influences the forecast using held-out matched KIV/ZOR prompt pairs. At layers located separately for each model, we swap the label-token hidden states (a patch) with those produced by the opposite-label prompt and regenerate the forecast. We perform this intervention both before and after City C provides direct observations.

\noindent\textbf{Finding: inferred relationships are represented internally and can shape forecasts.}
The decoded representation initially follows the relationship implied by the KIV/ZOR label, then shifts toward City C's actual response pattern as direct observations accumulate.
In Qwen3-14B, swapping this representation before City C provides any direct observations changes both the selected reference city and the numerical forecast, moving the forecast-implied response far enough that each arm lands close to where the opposite label began (Fig.~\ref{fig:coin-city-causal-patch}).
After four City C observations, the same intervention has no reliable effect.
The representation therefore influences the forecast when the model must rely on the inferred relationship, and yields to direct evidence once it identifies City C's response pattern. The same intervention in Llama-3.1-70B, at layers located for that model, is reported in
App.~\ref{app:coin-city-causal-patch}.

\noindent\textbf{Takeaway.}
Models can infer which predictive relationship applies, use it when direct evidence is sparse, and revise it as local evidence accumulates.
In stronger models, this behavior persists under arbitrary labels, appears in internal representations, and causally shapes the resulting forecast.
Experiment~3 asks whether training can carry this relationship use into new domains and response structures.

The original structure-shift row is a control: training contains only the direct
response, and every trained checkpoint emits zero delayed response. We therefore
rebuilt the task with both forms in training: two references have identical

follows. Three-seed Qwen3-8B RL learns marginal persistence without reliable cue

In a fixed component diagnostic crossing both domains and semantic or arbitrary
labels, all eight checkpoints identify the named reference perfectly.
Supplying exact response patterns does not rescue the 8B forecasts; an untrained
32B model shows partial numerical ability but often omits the resting baseline
(App.~\ref{app:exp3-components}). An exploratory supervised pilot on 4,800
prompts yields $97.9$--$100.0\%$ structure accuracy in Coin City/semantic at

labels or in Coin Harbor. Trained natively it reaches $100.0\%$ in Harbor/semantic yet
stays at chance under arbitrary labels: the barrier is the label mapping, not
the domain. Rate and budget differ, limiting this to in-domain learnability,
not a general SFT advantage or transferable induction (App.~\ref{app:exp3-learnability}).

\noindent\textbf{Composition accuracy and evidence use are distinct.}
On the mechanism-family benchmark, episode-matched Qwen3-4B training beats a
\emph{fixed population-prior} control on held-out compositions: response-MAE

episode level near-intact while destroying response shape, so the gain falls
where the manipulation bites; held-out gain tracking stays unestablished

\subsection{Exp.~4: external grounding in unseen historical markets}
\label{sec:exp4}

Historical markets test external forecasting accuracy without labeled mechanisms. We train on resolved markets using only
information available before each outcome, then evaluate 318 unseen questions
from different event families. Lower Brier scores mean less squared error
between forecast probabilities and realized outcomes
(App.~\ref{app:exp3b-protocol}).

Training lowers
error at every tested size, but the Qwen base-relative gap shrinks from 1.7B to
32B without steady improvement in trained accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:sealed-market-grounding}). At 32B, trained mean Brier is \(.1265\)
versus \(.1266\) for the base, the smallest observed gain; the trained-minus-base
interval \([-.00045,.00029]\) includes zero. A Llama 3B/8B
replication shows the same qualitative convergence pattern
(App. Figure~\ref{fig:exp4-llama-scale-results}). Several trained checkpoints
reach contemporaneous Polymarket performance. Hosted systems provide context,
not additional points on the local scaling curve.

\noindent\textbf{What Exp.~4 establishes.}
Outcome-based training improves mean forecasting accuracy on held-out event
families, with uncertain gains at the strongest Qwen base. This comparison is
trained versus base; without a shuffled-outcome control, it does not isolate the
contribution of learning episode-specific relationships. It complements the controlled transfer tests with external predictive validity.

\section{Related work: why accuracy is not enough}
\label{sec:related-work}

\noindent\textbf{Judgmental forecasting.}
Strong human forecasters combine base rates, decomposition, diverse evidence,
and repeated revision
\citep{tetlock2005expert,mellers2014psychological,tetlock2016superforecasting}.
LM benchmarks increasingly use time-indexed or leakage-controlled questions, and
retrieval or ensembling can bring systems near crowd accuracy
\citep{zou2022forecasting,karger2025forecastbench,halawi2024approaching,schoenegger2024wisdom}.
These results establish what probabilities models can produce. They do not tell
us what would make those probabilities change.

\noindent\textbf{Induction and relational reasoning.}
Transformers can infer functions from examples, and work on analogy and world
models asks whether learned representations capture relations or dynamics
\citep{garg2022what,chan2022data,webb2023emergent,yasunaga2024large,ha2018world,lecun2022path,li2023emergent}.
At the same time, shortcut research shows that surface success can fail under
structural change
\citep{geirhos2020shortcut,mitchell2023ai,vafa2025foundation}. A forecast can
therefore be right for the wrong reason: two models may report the same number
while relying on different populations, evidence, or pathways.

\noindent\textbf{Social agents and conditional validity.}
Generative agents can reproduce plausible individual and collective behavior
\citep{park2023generative,aher2023simulate,argyle2023out}, yet matching a
population average is not the same as preserving the relationships within that
population \citep{bisbee2024synthetic}. For systems intended to inform decisions,
we care about both the \emph{probability reported} and the \emph{relationship
that makes the evidence predictive}. Our experiments therefore move through
increasingly stringent tests: relevance and direction, contextual relationship
selection, revision from local evidence, induction of a novel convention, and
transfer across worlds. Each step rules out a weaker account of the same
forecast without assuming a general internal world model.

\section{Limitations: what these studies do and do not establish}
\label{sec:limitations}

These controlled tasks demonstrate context-dependent predictive relationships,
not human-like understanding or a general world model. Real settings contain
strategic interaction, unreliable cues, changing variables, and longer feedback
horizons absent from our market packets and constructed domains.

The internal intervention concerns one checkpoint and one convention; the
historical study covers one market cohort and training recipe. Additional
architectures, later cohorts, interactive settings, and misleading evidence
would test generality.

Performance is heterogeneous. Smaller checkpoints often struggle to induce the
KIV/ZOR meaning. The episode-matching advantage under joint domain and mechanism
shift is bounded below about $.13$ by the eight-seed roster rather than
resolved; the benefit for compositions
of familiar mechanisms is checkpoint-specific, and against a
distribution-matched shuffled-target control its sign is stable at seven seeds
but its separation from zero is not. Structure diagnostics use no target
observations; the supervised pilot replicates at four seeds but differs from
RL in update budget and, at that setting, learning rate. Semantic cue-to-pattern
learning could explain its in-domain success; native training places its
boundary at labels, not domain. The five-seed gain intervention establishes
evidence use on seen but not held-out mechanisms.
Historical-market gains narrow at the strongest Qwen base. The nonmonotonic
scale comparison uses one recipe; larger models may require different
optimization or data budgets.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Forecast accuracy alone does not establish inductive reasoning. Models select
relevant evidence and revise contextual relationships, while training improves
forecasts across domains and held-out markets. A supervised pilot learns
response-form selection in distribution; its boundary is arbitrary labels, not
domain. Composition accuracy favors matched training in sign without
establishing transferred evidence use. These contrasts distinguish predictive accuracy from
reliable relationship use without presuming a general world model.
